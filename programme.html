How the programme could work.

Phases, formats, participation, intellectual property, publication and governance for a proposed Oxford Cancer AI Challenges programme.

From research problem to reviewed challenge

Our current focus is Phase 1: collecting research challenges. Researchers do not need to define a complete competition. Promising proposals would be developed with their owners before participants are invited.

  1. 1

    Collect challenges — current focus

    Invite Oxford researchers, clinicians and research groups to describe specific problems that could benefit from AI, computational methods or new collaborations.

  2. 2

    Match samples, evidence & compute

    Refine the clinical brief with its owner. Approach industry, biobanks and research facilities about sample access, assays, reference evidence, data and compute. Agree feasibility, permissions, evaluation and responsibilities.

  3. 3

    Invite the AI community

    Once the brief, resources and approvals are in place, advertise the challenge to AI researchers, developers, universities and industry. Publish eligibility, the baseline, evaluation and participation terms.

  4. 4

    Build, validate & continue

    Bring teams together for a hackathon or longer research competition, with scientific office hours and practical support. Evaluate independently, review with clinical experts and agree any follow-on work.

The challenge pack

A concrete starting point for every team.

Prepared with the owner before recruitment. A small hackathon might start with a curated subset; a longer competition may need a substantial evidence and validation package.

  • 01 A scientific owner and a specific clinical or research objective
  • 02 An agreed data or sample route, with permitted uses and access arrangements
  • 03 A starter baseline, reference outputs and independent evaluation plan
  • 04 A named support route and realistic compute or assay allocation
  • 05 A reproducible submission format, review process and agreed credit

Choose a format to fit the question.

Timeframes follow the evidence, permissions and support available. These are possible formats, not scheduled events.

Short hackathon

A focused prototype

A few days for a curated data task, a reproducible workflow, an assay design or a well-specified device concept. Wet-lab proof is not assumed achievable during a weekend.

Research competition

Time to build and evaluate

Several weeks or months for a defined model task, clinical office hours, independent evaluation and an agreed assay or compute allocation.

Longer collaboration

Develop the evidence base

A cohort, new reference data or prospective experimental work may require a separate funded collaboration before a challenge can open.

Frequently asked questions

Who can participate?

The programme aims to welcome participants from Oxford and the wider academic and industry communities, including academic researchers, students, clinicians and technology providers. Eligibility and participation requirements will be specified for each challenge. We are currently collecting research problems; participant recruitment would follow approval.

Who owns the intellectual property?

Participation does not automatically transfer ownership of intellectual property to the organisers. Participants and challenge owners retain their existing rights, subject to their institutional agreements, applicable licences and any specific terms agreed before a challenge begins. Where necessary, additional agreements may be established for data access, infrastructure use or collaboration.

Are separate agreements required?

Some challenges may require data-use agreements, confidentiality arrangements or agreements with infrastructure providers. These will be communicated before participation. All data must be used lawfully, ethically and in accordance with relevant consent, approvals and access conditions. Responsibilities will be defined between the relevant owners, organisers, institutions, providers and participants; they do not rest solely with participants.

Will my contribution be acknowledged?

Challenge owners and contributors will be acknowledged publicly with their permission. We aim to recognise contributions from researchers who propose challenges, provide data, develop methods or support the work.

Can challenge results be published?

We encourage participants to share their methods and results where permitted. Promising projects may lead to further collaborations or scientific publications, but publication is not guaranteed. Any publication plans must respect applicable data-use agreements, confidentiality requirements and institutional policies.

How will authorship and credit be decided?

Authorship will be based on actual contributions and relevant scientific publishing guidelines. It will be discussed and agreed between collaborators rather than automatically assigned through participation. Where appropriate, challenge-specific arrangements for publication, acknowledgement and use of results should be established before work begins.

Can teams continue working on a project after the hackathon?

Yes, where all relevant parties agree. We hope that some challenges will lead to longer-term research collaborations. Any further use of data, infrastructure or intellectual property will be subject to the necessary permissions and agreements.

Does proposing a challenge mean releasing my data?

No. Challenge owners retain control over whether and under what approved conditions their data can be used. Access would be discussed before any data are used. Public resources, controlled access, secure environments, federated approaches or synthetic development data may be explored; no service or access route is promised.

Do I need a complete technical specification?

No. Describe a specific scientific problem even if the AI approach, dataset, duration, evaluation method or computing requirements are not yet clear. Choose “Not sure — happy to discuss” where appropriate. Organisers would work with owners to refine promising proposals.

How much time will a challenge take?

A short hackathon could produce a focused prototype over a few days. A larger research competition could run for weeks or months and involve more substantial data preparation and evaluation. The appropriate format and commitment would be agreed with the challenge owner during review; no schedule is confirmed.

Do teams need their own computing resources?

Possible resource needs would be scoped during review. Sample access, assays, compute, credits, secure environments and provider support are not currently committed. Any agreed support, access conditions and resource limits would be explained before a challenge begins.

Can we use AI tools?

The intended approach welcomes suitable AI and computational methods. Any restrictions on tools, external services or data transfers would be set by the agreed data conditions and challenge rules. Teams would document the methods and tools used.

Are there prizes or guaranteed publications?

No prizes, funding or publications are committed. Any awards or dissemination arrangements would be stated for an approved challenge before participation, alongside the agreed credit and authorship principles.

Key terms

Short explanations of the cancer and data terms used on this page, for people joining from other fields.

Epitope
A molecular feature recognised by the immune system. The proposed vaccine challenge focuses on tumour-derived peptides and their immune recognition.
Neoantigen
A tumour-associated antigen arising from a change such as a mutation. A predicted candidate still needs appropriate experimental evidence.
HLA and peptide presentation
Human leukocyte antigen molecules display peptides to immune cells. Presentation, T-cell recognition and vaccine benefit are distinct questions.
Biomarker
A measurable sign in the body, such as a gene change or blood protein, that gives information about disease or likely treatment response.
Cancer stage
How far a cancer has grown or spread. Stage 1 is usually small and contained. Stage 4 has spread to other parts of the body.
Liquid biopsy
A test that looks for signs of cancer in a body fluid, usually blood, instead of a tissue sample.
Cell-free DNA (cfDNA)
Short fragments of DNA circulating outside cells in blood. Most come from healthy cells as they die and are replaced.
Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA)
The part of cell-free DNA that comes from a tumour. In early disease it can be a tiny fraction of the total.
Multi-cancer early detection (MCED)
A single test, usually on blood, designed to look for signals from many types of cancer at once.
Plasma
The liquid part of blood, left once the blood cells and platelets have been removed. Most liquid biopsies analyse it.
Methylation
Chemical marks on DNA that help control which genes are active. Their patterns differ between tissues and change in cancer.
Fragmentomics
The study of cfDNA fragment patterns, such as length and where fragments begin and end, which can differ in cancer.
Copy number change
A gain or loss of sections of DNA, compared with the usual two copies. These changes are common in tumours.
WGS, WGBS and shallow sequencing
WGS reads DNA across the whole genome. WGBS also captures methylation. Shallow sequencing reads less of each region, which lowers the cost.
Long-read sequencing
Methods such as nanopore that can read long DNA molecules in one pass. Most cfDNA tests use short reads.
Sensitivity
The share of people with cancer whom a test correctly flags. Missed cancers lower sensitivity.
Specificity
The share of people without cancer whom a test correctly clears. False alarms lower specificity.
H&E staining
Standard dyes, haematoxylin and eosin, that make the structure of cells and tissue visible on a pathology slide.
Spatial transcriptomics
Methods that measure which genes are active in a tissue sample and where they are active.
Tumour microenvironment
The cancer cells together with nearby immune cells, blood vessels and supporting tissue.
Checkpoint inhibitor
A type of immunotherapy that blocks signals tumours use to hold back immune cells.
PD-L1 and tumour mutational burden
Two biomarkers used to guide immunotherapy. PD-L1 is a protein on cells. Tumour mutational burden counts mutations in the tumour.
Plasma proteomics
Measuring many proteins in plasma at once. Platforms such as Olink can measure thousands from one small sample.
Held-out data
Data hidden from teams while they build their models, and used only to score them. It gives a fairer test of real performance.
Research context and cancer statistics

Why cancer research needs this

Cancer survival has improved, but outcomes still vary widely. AI and computational collaboration could support prevention, cancer biology, diagnosis, treatment, drug discovery and research workflows. We expect multiple themes, identified through discussions with Oxford research groups, clinicians and PIs. The examples below are starting points, not a final or approved list.

403,601

new cancer cases a year in the UK (2019, 2021 to 2022)

~170,000

cancer deaths a year in the UK (2022 to 2024)

49.8%

of people diagnosed in the UK are predicted to survive for 10 years or more (2018). This ranges from 11.1% for lung cancer to 76.6% for breast cancer in women.

One possible theme: finding cancer earlier

Earlier diagnosis can mean more treatment options and better outcomes. The NHS Long Term Plan set an ambition for 75% of cancers to be diagnosed at stage 1 or 2 by 2028.

Blood tests could help, but early tumours release very little DNA. One commentary reports that even for larger tumours, tumour DNA averages about 0.1% of DNA fragments in blood, or 1 in every 1,000. Very small tumours may release much less, so a test has to find a faint signal among healthy DNA.

The chart shows this in practice. SYMPLIFY, an Oxford-sponsored study, tested one commercial blood test in 5,461 people with symptoms who were referred for cancer investigation in England and Wales. The test detected most stage 3 and 4 cancers, and about a quarter of those at stage 1.

A discussion draft, with a clear route to review.

The immediate aim is to identify useful clinical research challenges with Oxford PIs, clinicians and research groups. Wider recruitment would follow scoping and approval.

This draft can be shared by direct link for PI feedback. GitHub Pages remains the current hosting route; it is publicly reachable, not an access-controlled place for confidential information. A later move to OxCODE would require approval.

Before public launch or wider advertising, organisational approval must cover names, logos and descriptions of individual research proposals. Data governance, a working submission route, contact arrangements and a privacy notice also need to be established. No researcher or organisation is represented as endorsing this proposal.
