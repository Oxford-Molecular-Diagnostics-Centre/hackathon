Oxford Cancer AI Challenges

Real cancer research problems, published as open AI challenges.

Oxford cancer teams and clinicians publish a research need. AI researchers form teams around it, and infrastructure providers back the work. Each challenge runs to its own timetable.

Why cancer research needs this

Cancer survival has improved, but outcomes still vary widely between cancers. Many of the hardest problems are now also data problems, and they need people from different fields working on them together.

Early cancers are the hardest to find

Earlier diagnosis can mean more treatment options and better outcomes. The NHS Long Term Plan set an ambition for 75% of cancers to be diagnosed at stage 1 or 2 by 2028.

Blood tests could help, but early tumours release very little DNA. One commentary reports that even for larger tumours, tumour DNA averages about 0.1% of DNA fragments in blood, or 1 in every 1,000. Very small tumours may release much less, so a test has to find a faint signal among healthy DNA.

The chart shows this in practice. SYMPLIFY, an Oxford-sponsored study, tested one commercial blood test in 5,461 people with symptoms who were referred for cancer investigation in England and Wales. The test detected most stage 3 and 4 cancers, and about a quarter of those at stage 1.

Three groups, one shared problem

Cancer research teams often face similar data and AI problems in separate institutions and disciplines. Each challenge gives them a reason to work together on something specific.

How a challenge runs

The same five steps apply to a two-day sprint and to a year-long benchmark. Only the timetable changes.

  1. 1

    Publish the need

    A research team writes a short brief covering the problem, data, metric and timetable.

  2. 2

    Match infrastructure

    Providers offer resources against that brief, matched to the data's access conditions.

  3. 3

    Register teams

    Teams sign up for the challenge. Mixed teams are encouraged, and solo participants can be matched.

  4. 4

    Run the window

    Teams work during that challenge's own dates, with access to the people who know the data.

  5. 5

    Score and share

    Entries are scored on held-out data. Results, methods and reusable tools are published.

Supported track

Selected research teams

Teams apply for allocated compute or credits when a provider has offered them.

Community track

Individuals and small teams

Open registration with shared resources where available, and matching for solo participants.

Open track

Self-supported teams

Any eligible team can enter with its own compute, wherever the data's terms allow it.

Long-running benchmark · 12 months

An open benchmark for multi-cancer blood tests

A multi-cancer early detection test looks for signals from many cancers in one blood sample. These tests are usually evaluated on different cohorts, with different cancer types, stages and patient groups, so their results are hard to compare directly. Large patient datasets also tend to stay private, which makes new methods harder to build.

This benchmark would create a shared dataset and a fair public ranking, as ImageNet's ILSVRC did for image classification and CASP did for protein structure prediction.

What does sensitivity at 95% specificity mean? It asks how many cancers a test finds if it may raise a false alarm in only 5 of every 100 people without cancer. False alarms matter because they can lead to anxiety and unnecessary investigations.

Detect cancer earlier from a blood sample

A liquid biopsy looks for traces of a tumour in blood instead of taking tissue. These challenges aim to find a faint signal reliably, without too many false alarms.

Combine blood DNA signals into one cancer test

Mixed data accessOpen window · 8 weeks
Why it matters

Tumours mark the DNA fragments they release in several ways, including methylation, fragment length, copy number changes and mutations. Any one signal can be too faint in early disease. Oxford's TriOx test combined several signals and reported 94.9% sensitivity and 88.8% specificity in an early study across six cancer types, in people with symptoms. Larger studies are still needed.

TriOx study, Nature Communications 2025
The challenge
Build a scalable pipeline that combines these signals and classifies cancer versus no cancer.
How to score it
Sensitivity at 95% specificity, reported by cancer type and stage.
Data route
FinaleDB, Cell-free Epigenome Atlas, GSE213890, DELFI

Find the tumour DNA stored in platelets

Needs a data partner or approvalOpen window · 10 weeks
Why it matters

Blood clears loose DNA quickly, and early tumours release very little. Oxford researchers showed that platelets, the cells best known for clotting, take up DNA fragments, including DNA from cancer and precancerous bowel cells. Current tests usually discard platelets. One researcher suggests that including them might even double DNA yield, though this is at a very early stage.

Cancer Research UK news on the Oxford study, 2025
The challenge
Measure how much useful signal platelet DNA adds, and build analysis methods suited to it.
How to score it
Added sensitivity over plasma alone, on matched samples.
Data route
Paired platelet and plasma samples, from a contributing lab

Tune nanopore analysis for cell-free DNA

Mixed data accessOpen window · 8 weeks
Why it matters

Nanopore sequencers read DNA of any length and are portable. In a small study of lung and bladder cancer, tumour fractions correlated strongly with short-read results (R = 0.98), and results could be available within 24 hours. Nanopore also recovered far more long fragments. Lower single-read accuracy still limits the detection of small mutations.

Nanopore cfDNA study, EMBO Molecular Medicine 2023
The challenge
Compare basecalling and analysis methods on short fragments, then combine copy number, fragment and methylation signals in one workflow.
How to score it
Accuracy against known reference data, then signal detection on cancer samples.
Data route
Oxford Nanopore Open Data for development, controlled cancer cohorts for testing

Make liquid biopsy data work together

Open data availableSprint · 2–3 days
Why it matters

Studies differ in how blood is collected and processed, how deeply it is sequenced, and how patients and cancer stages are recorded. These differences make cohorts hard to combine, and can look like cancer signals to a model. The BloodPAC consortium has built a shared data commons in response to this problem.

BloodPAC Data Commons, 2021
The challenge
Build a tool that converts mismatched cfDNA study files and metadata into one common format.
How to score it
Mapping accuracy against hand-curated records from unseen studies.
Data route
FinaleDB, Cell-free Epigenome Atlas, BloodPAC metadata

Read more from tumour tissue

A tumour is a mix of cancer, immune and supporting cells. How those cells are arranged can influence how the disease behaves and responds to treatment.

Read cell populations from routine pathology slides

Open data availableOpen window · 6 weeks
Why it matters

Pathologists routinely examine tumour tissue on slides stained with H&E. Spatial transcriptomics shows which genes are active at each location, revealing far more about the tumour microenvironment, but its cost has limited wider use. Predicting that information from ordinary slides could extend it to existing archives. Current models still need to become more accurate and reliable across datasets.

HEST-1k dataset and benchmark
The challenge
Predict gene expression and cell-type composition from H&E images.
How to score it
Pearson correlation on unseen spatial samples, building on HEST-Benchmark.
Data route
HEST-1k, TCGA slides through Imaging Data Commons

Recover single-cell detail from spatial data

Open data availableOpen window · 6 weeks
Why it matters

Many spatial methods measure small spots that each contain several cells, which blends their signals. Rare but important cells, such as immune cells at a tumour's edge, can be averaged away. Recovering single-cell profiles could reveal these cells in data that has already been collected.

HEST-1k dataset and benchmark
The challenge
Recover cell-level expression from multi-cell spots, working from high-resolution samples binned down to coarse spots.
How to score it
Similarity to the original high-resolution profiles, which teams do not see.
Data route
Xenium and Visium HD samples in HEST-1k

Predict risk and treatment response

Better predictions could help focus prevention on the people most at risk, and treatment on the people most likely to benefit.

Predict cancer risk from blood proteins

Needs a data partner or approvalOpen window · 12 weeks
Why it matters

Blood proteins can change long before diagnosis. A UK Biobank study of 44,645 people found 618 links between proteins and cancers, including 107 for cancers diagnosed more than seven years after the blood sample. Oxford researchers also found proteins linked to prostate cancer risk more than 15 years ahead. These findings are not yet a usable screening test.

Proteomic risk factors, Nature Communications 2024
The challenge
Build a risk model from plasma proteins, adding genetic and clinical data where permitted.
How to score it
Discrimination and calibration on an independent cohort.
Data route
UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project, for approved researchers

Predict who will benefit from immunotherapy

Open data availableOpen window · 8 weeks
Why it matters

Checkpoint inhibitors help the immune system attack cancer and can produce lasting responses, but only for some patients. One upper-bound estimate for the US in 2018 suggested that about 44% of people with cancer were eligible and about 12.5% would respond. Biomarkers such as PD-L1 and tumour mutational burden are imperfect guides. Better prediction could spare others side effects and lost time.

Haslam and Prasad, JAMA Network Open 2019
The challenge
Combine biomarkers from public trial cohorts to predict checkpoint inhibitor response.
How to score it
Performance on a cohort left out entirely, testing whether a model generalises.
Data route
TIGER, CRI iAtlas, TIDE

Design better medicines

Many cancer drugs work by binding to a specific protein. Better predictions could help researchers decide which compounds to make and test.

Predict how compounds bind their targets

Open data availableOpen window · 6 weeks
Why it matters

Predicting how a compound binds its protein target helps researchers choose what to make and test. Models are limited by the shortage of consistent, high-quality binding data. OpenBind, co-founded by Oxford and Diamond Light Source, aims to produce more than 500,000 structures and measurements over five years. Its first release covers a viral protein, so cancer targets would follow as data allows.

OpenBind first release, Diamond Light Source
The challenge
Improve binding prediction using standardised open structure and affinity data.
How to score it
Pose and affinity accuracy on unseen compounds.
Data route
OpenBind EV-A71 2A protease release

Key terms

      
Short explanations of the cancer and data terms used on this page, for people joining from other fields.

    

    
Biomarker
A measurable sign in the body, such as a gene change or blood protein, that gives information about disease or likely treatment response.
Cancer stage
How far a cancer has grown or spread. Stage 1 is usually small and contained. Stage 4 has spread to other parts of the body.
Liquid biopsy
A test that looks for signs of cancer in a body fluid, usually blood, instead of a tissue sample.
Cell-free DNA (cfDNA)
Short fragments of DNA circulating outside cells in blood. Most come from healthy cells as they die and are replaced.
Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA)
The part of cell-free DNA that comes from a tumour. In early disease it can be a tiny fraction of the total.
Multi-cancer early detection (MCED)
A single test, usually on blood, designed to look for signals from many types of cancer at once.
Plasma
The liquid part of blood, left once the blood cells and platelets have been removed. Most liquid biopsies analyse it.
Methylation
Chemical marks on DNA that help control which genes are active. Their patterns differ between tissues and change in cancer.
Fragmentomics
The study of cfDNA fragment patterns, such as length and where fragments begin and end, which can differ in cancer.
Copy number change
A gain or loss of sections of DNA, compared with the usual two copies. These changes are common in tumours.
WGS, WGBS and shallow sequencing
WGS reads DNA across the whole genome. WGBS also captures methylation. Shallow sequencing reads less of each region, which lowers the cost.
Long-read sequencing
Methods such as nanopore that can read long DNA molecules in one pass. Most cfDNA tests use short reads.
Sensitivity
The share of people with cancer whom a test correctly flags. Missed cancers lower sensitivity.
Specificity
The share of people without cancer whom a test correctly clears. False alarms lower specificity.
H&E staining
Standard dyes, haematoxylin and eosin, that make the structure of cells and tissue visible on a pathology slide.
Spatial transcriptomics
Methods that measure which genes are active in a tissue sample and where they are active.
Tumour microenvironment
The cancer cells together with nearby immune cells, blood vessels and supporting tissue.
Checkpoint inhibitor
A type of immunotherapy that blocks signals tumours use to hold back immune cells.
PD-L1 and tumour mutational burden
Two biomarkers used to guide immunotherapy. PD-L1 is a protein on cells. Tumour mutational burden counts mutations in the tumour.
Plasma proteomics
Measuring many proteins in plasma at once. Platforms such as Olink can measure thousands from one small sample.
Held-out data
Data hidden from teams while they build their models, and used only to score them. It gives a fairer test of real performance.

  






  

    
Frequently asked questions

    
Who can take part?
Cancer researchers, clinicians, data scientists, software engineers and students can take part, alone or in a team. Each brief states any eligibility conditions, including those set by its data.
Do I need my own computing resources?
Not necessarily. Each brief lists any resources available from infrastructure partners. Teams with their own compute can enter through the open track.
Who owns the results?
Each brief states the data and output licences before teams register. Open code and methods are the default, subject to any data or funder terms.
Can we use AI tools?
Yes, including coding assistants and large language models, unless a data agreement prevents it. Teams should describe the tools they used when they submit.
How much time will it take?
Each challenge has its own timeframe, from a two-to-three-day sprint to a year-long benchmark. Dates appear in each brief when confirmed.
Do I need a cancer research background?
No. Mixed teams are encouraged, and each challenge explains its clinical background. Challenge owners and clinical advisers will help teams interpret the problem.
How is patient data protected?
Early challenges use public data. Controlled data stays under its existing approvals and is used only in an approved environment. Never submit patient data through this site.
Can companies take part?
This depends on the data. Some datasets, including HEST-1k, have non-commercial licences. Each brief states who can enter.
Are there prizes?
The first challenges offer recognition, public credit and invitations to present, without cash prizes. Any longer-term benchmark may set its own awards.
